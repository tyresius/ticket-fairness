# Keep the Fee Cap. Fix the Ceiling.

**Why a face-value resale cap will not work, and what will**
Patrick Tyler · independent researcher · [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/bytelawyer/) · August 2026

## Summary for policymakers

The Government has got the hard half of this policy right and the easy half
wrong.

Capping platform service fees is genuinely good policy, and the modelling
behind this submission confirms it delivers a real consumer gain. But
pairing it with a **face-value resale ceiling** throws away most of the
benefit — and repeats a mistake that has now failed in three jurisdictions.

Setting the ceiling at **10–20% above face value instead of zero, with
every other element of the draft Bill left intact**, roughly doubles the
fan-welfare gain. It does so while cutting the professional touting margin
by 71–89% at the demand levels where touting actually happens.

The reason is not complicated, and it is not really about economics. It is
that a resale market is not a nuisance attached to the ticket market. It is
part of how the ticket market works — and if you make the lawful version
unusable, you do not eliminate resale. You relocate it somewhere nobody can
see it.

## Why this keeps happening

Ticket resale reform has an unusual political property: **it destroys its
own coalition at the moment it succeeds.**

Before tickets go on sale, every fan wants the same thing. Nobody has a
ticket, everybody is exposed to touts, and support for reform is close to
universal. The moment tickets are allocated, that unified public splits in
two. The fans who got tickets now hold something valuable, and their
interest quietly changes: they want flexibility to resell, protection of
what they hold, and no new restrictions. The fans who missed out want the
rules torn up.

This is the endowment effect operating at the level of a political
constituency, and it is why "fans" cannot be treated as one group with one
interest. Reform is designed for the people standing in the queue, and then
enforced on a population where half of them have already sat down.

Almost every analysis of ticket policy — including the Government's —
models fans as a single representative consumer with a single preference.
That assumption is what produces face-value caps. If fans are one group who
simply want cheap tickets, then the cheapest possible ceiling must be the
best one. Our modelling separates fans into distinct types with genuinely
opposed interests, and the result is different: the cheapest ceiling is not
the best one, because it destroys the mechanism that gets tickets to the
fans who missed the on-sale.

## Touts cannot be banned. They can only be moved.

The instinct behind a face-value cap is that touts are parasites and the
correct number of them is zero. The evidence does not support this, and
neither does the economics.

Peer-reviewed work on this exact market (Leslie and Sorensen, *Review of
Economic Studies*, 2014) finds that resale genuinely improves allocation —
tickets move from people who value them less to people who value them more,
worth around 5% in efficiency. But roughly a third of that gain is eaten by
**rent-seeking in the primary market**: professional brokers spending real
resources to reach inventory first. Resale is neither costless nor
worthless. That is an argument for *bounding* the resale margin, not for
abolishing it.

Bound it too tightly and the function does not stop. It moves. Ontario's
face-value cap came into force on 31 July 2026. A major platform delisted
its Ontario resale inventory, and within days reporting documented sellers
moving to general classified sites, listing tickets at a nominal one dollar
on an "ask-first" basis and negotiating the real price privately by
message. That is the same transaction as before, minus the audit trail,
minus the chargeback rights, minus any prospect of enforcement.

Ireland offers the longer view. Its face-value cap has been law since July
2021. In five years it has produced **no prosecutions** — a point raised in
the Oireachtas in May 2026, and one that does not depend on any industry
source. A rule that cannot be enforced is not a protection; it is a
redirection.

Survey evidence from Ireland and Victoria also reports materially higher
ticket-fraud rates than the pre-ban UK market. We treat that evidence
cautiously and say so plainly: it was commissioned by a secondary-market
operator, its own authors state that causal inference is not possible from
the data, and different polling panels were used in each country. We cite
it as consistent with the mechanism, not as proof of it. The Ontario
behaviour and the Irish enforcement record do not carry those caveats.

## What we did differently

The question this submission tries to answer is not "what do fans want."
It is: **would you accept these rules without knowing which participant you
would be — and knowing that your interests will change once you find out?**

That is a deliberately harder test than the standard one. Rawls asks what
rules you would choose from behind a veil of ignorance about your own
position. This adds the part that ticket markets make unavoidable: your
preferences do not stay fixed after the veil lifts. The fan who gets a
ticket becomes a different political actor from the fan who does not. A
rule that is only stable while everyone is still queueing is not a stable
rule.

We built an agent-based simulation of the primary market, the lawful resale
market, and the unlawful channel, populated by four participant types whose
behaviour is anchored to published evidence — including professional
resellers who enter and exit depending on whether the margin is worth their
costs, and who compete harder for primary inventory when resale is more
profitable. Then we scored every candidate rule from the standpoint of each
participant type, and asked which rules survive the test above.

## What the evidence says

**A face-value ceiling makes lawful resale loss-making.** Secondary
platform fees run to roughly 30% of ticket price on both sides combined. A
fan reselling at face value therefore recovers less than they paid. When
the lawful channel is loss-making by construction, supply leaves it. A
positive ceiling is not a concession to touts; it is the minimum condition
for a lawful resale market to exist at all.

**The ceiling is the stronger of the two levers.** Against the current
market, the fee cap alone improves population-weighted fan welfare by
+0.06 to +0.27 on our index. Adding a 20% ceiling to the same fee cap
improves it by +0.08 to +0.25 — **a median of 2.13× the gain from the fee
cap alone.** The relationship is not uniform, and we should be explicit
about that: the ceiling's added value ranges from 2.5× at mildly
oversubscribed events down to roughly parity at one demand level (4×,
where the fee cap alone happens to perform unusually well). The two
measures reinforce each other across almost the whole range, and the fee
cap is worth keeping on its own. But at the high-demand events that
generate the touting problem in the first place, it is the ceiling that
does the heavier work.

**A 10–20% ceiling still destroys the touting business.** This is the
objection we expected and tested directly. Compared with the current
unregulated market, a 20% ceiling cuts professional reseller margin by
**71–89% at the demand levels where touting is worth doing** (events five
to ten times oversubscribed). It does not cut it as completely as a
face-value cap does — that would be untrue and we do not claim it. It cuts
it far enough that industrial-scale touting stops being a business, while
leaving an ordinary fan able to pass on a ticket without losing money. We
should also note the limit of this finding: at events only mildly
oversubscribed, a 20% ceiling barely binds at all, because there is little
margin to extract in the first place.

**Leakage responds to the price gap, not the fee rules.** Capping platform
fees leaves off-platform routing essentially unchanged in our runs, because
what drives a transaction off-platform is the gap between the ceiling and
what someone will pay. A modest positive ceiling narrows that gap. A fee
cap does not touch it.

This last point is where the Government's own analysis is silent. The
analytical note accompanying the consultation response assumes a **20%
displacement effect** — touts continuing to sell above the cap outside
regulated platforms — while stating that this assumption "lacks strong
evidence." The £112 million annual saving is calculated carefully on the
price side. The behavioural response on the supply side, which determines
whether that saving materialises at all, is assumed. We do not claim to
have settled that question. We do claim it is the decisive one, and that it
can be modelled explicitly rather than guessed.

## The recommendation

Keep every enforcement element of the draft Bill — the platform fee cap,
the platform duty to monitor, the resale quantity limit, the CMA's new
powers. Change one number: set the resale ceiling in the **10–20% band
above face value** rather than at face value.

This is not an exotic request, on two counts. The *Putting Fans First*
consultation put four capped options to respondents — no uplift, 10% or
less, 10–20%, and 20–30% — and stated that the Government had "no favoured
approach at this stage." The recommendation here is that the evidence
supports the middle of the range the Government itself consulted on,
rather than its strictest end.

It is also no longer hypothetical. In July 2026 the Council of the District
of Columbia unanimously passed the RESALE Act, which caps resale at **10%
above face value alongside a 10% cap on fees** — the same two-lever design
the draft Bill proposes, but with the ceiling set above zero.

That pairing — a bounded non-zero ceiling combined with a cap on platform
fees — is the structure this model identified on its own, from simulation,
before the DC result was known to us. We claim no influence on it, and the
convergence is the point: two methods, one arrived at by legislative
process and one by modelling, reached the same design and differ only on
where the ceiling sits.

On that question the model is direct. Holding fee rules identical and
varying **only** the ceiling, a 10% ceiling outperforms a face-value
ceiling on population-weighted welfare at **every oversubscribed demand
level we tested** (paired runs, N=250–300 per point; all differences
statistically significant). The result holds when the resale-option term is
removed from the index entirely, when DC's actual 10% fee structure is used
in place of our original assumption, and when the face-value arm is given a
tighter quantity limit and stronger enforcement than we first modelled.

Two honest qualifications. First, this is a controlled comparison of
ceiling level, not a head-to-head of two legislative packages: several
provisions on each side — DC's speculative-sale ban and reseller
registration, the draft Bill's platform monitoring duty and CMA powers —
are outside the model. Second, the advantage is specific to the ceiling.
If a face-value regime instead tightened its **fee** cap to around 2%, the
ceiling advantage narrows sharply and reverses at moderate demand. The
case for a positive ceiling is strong but it is not unconditional, and we
would rather state the condition than have it found.

**A band rather than a single figure, for a substantive reason.** The
best-performing ceiling is not the same for every event. Across
oversubscribed events our runs put it between 10% and 30%, and above
roughly three times oversubscribed it rises steadily with scarcity: around
10% for a moderately hot event, around 20% at five times demand, and around
30% for the most extreme cases. The logic is straightforward — the greater
the gap between the ceiling and what someone will pay, the stronger the
pull off-platform, so the hottest events need a slightly higher ceiling to
keep resale lawful and visible. This is a finding, not a hedge: it means any
single national number is necessarily wrong for some class of event, and a
face-value cap is furthest wrong precisely for the stadium-scale events that
generate the most touting and the most public anger. We recommend 10–20% as
a starting band because it performs close to the optimum across the range of
events a general rule has to cover; a Government minded to set one figure
should understand which events it is choosing to fit.

A ceiling in that band keeps fan-to-fan resale lawful and viable, keeps it
on platforms where the new monitoring duty can actually operate, and leaves
professional touting without a business model. A face-value ceiling
achieves the last of those and forfeits the first two — and, on the
evidence from Ireland and now Ontario, does not reliably achieve even the
third.

## About the evidence

Agent-based simulation of primary, lawful-resale, and unlawful channels:
four buyer and reseller types with empirically anchored behaviour, platform
fees on both sides of resale, enforcement frictions, and professional
resellers entering and exiting in response to expected margin. Sixteen
rulesets × ten demand levels × 100 seeded runs per point; results are
bit-for-bit reproducible from the published code and seed. Robustness: all
33 model parameters perturbed simultaneously (Latin-hypercube, N=400); the
bounded-ceiling advantage over the current market survives in 96–99% of
draws, and no single parameter can be tuned to reverse it. Source code,
data, sensitivity analyses, and a documented limitations ledger — including
scope exclusions such as presale allocations and multi-event dynamics — are
published for scrutiny.

Two limitations deserve stating here rather than in an appendix. First,
this is a simulation calibrated to published evidence, not a field
experiment; it establishes a mechanism and its direction, not a precise
elasticity. Second, the welfare index is a constructed measure, and the
optimal ceiling within the 10–30% range shifts depending on which
participants you weight most heavily. What does not shift, across every
weighting we tested, is that a bounded positive ceiling outperforms both
the current market and a face-value cap.
